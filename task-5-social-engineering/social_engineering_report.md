# Social Engineering Attacks Report

## Introduction

Social engineering is a cyberattack technique in which attackers manipulate people into revealing confidential information or performing actions that compromise security. Unlike technical attacks that target software vulnerabilities, social engineering exploits human psychology such as trust, fear, urgency, curiosity, and authority. According to cybersecurity awareness studies, phishing remains one of the most common initial attack vectors used in data breaches and ransomware incidents. Because human behavior is difficult to control completely, social engineering continues to be highly effective.

---

# 1. Phishing

## What is Phishing?

Phishing is a fraudulent attempt to obtain sensitive information such as passwords, banking credentials, or one-time passwords by pretending to be a trustworthy entity through email, SMS, phone calls, or websites.

## Types of Phishing

### Spear Phishing

Targets a specific individual or organization using personalized information.

### Whaling

Targets senior executives or high-value individuals.

### Vishing

Voice phishing conducted through phone calls.

### Smishing

SMS phishing conducted through text messages.

## How Phishing Works

1. The attacker creates a fake message.
2. The victim receives the message and is urged to act quickly.
3. The victim clicks a malicious link or opens an attachment.
4. Credentials or sensitive information are stolen.

## Real-World Case Study: Twitter 2020 Attack

In July 2020, attackers used social engineering against Twitter employees to gain access to internal systems. Several high-profile accounts, including those of public figures and companies, were compromised and used to post cryptocurrency scam messages. The incident demonstrated that convincing employees can be more effective than exploiting software vulnerabilities.

## Prevention Recommendations

* Verify sender email addresses carefully.
* Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA).
* Avoid clicking links in unsolicited messages.
* Conduct regular phishing awareness training.
* Report suspicious emails immediately.

# 2. Pretexting

## Definition

Pretexting is a social engineering technique in which an attacker invents a believable story or identity to obtain information or access.

## How Attackers Build a False Scenario

Attackers may pretend to be:

* IT support staff,
* bank representatives,
* HR personnel,
* auditors,
* or government officials.

They often gather background information from social media or public sources before contacting the victim.

## Case Study

A common corporate pretexting scenario involves an attacker calling an employee while pretending to be from the IT department and requesting a password reset code for a "system upgrade." Employees who trust the caller may unknowingly provide authentication information.

## Prevention Measures

* Verify identity through official communication channels.
* Never share passwords or authentication codes over phone or email.
* Follow documented verification procedures before granting access.

---

# 3. Baiting

## Definition

Baiting involves offering something attractive to entice a victim into performing an action that compromises security.

## Physical Baiting

Examples include infected USB drives left in parking lots, offices, or public areas.

## Digital Baiting

Examples include fake software downloads, pirated content, free games, or malicious mobile applications.

## Case Study

Security experiments have shown that many employees plug found USB drives into workplace computers out of curiosity. If the device contains malware, it can automatically install malicious software and provide attackers access to the network.

## Prevention Measures

* Do not connect unknown USB devices.
* Restrict USB device usage through organizational policies.
* Download software only from trusted official sources.

---

# 4. Quid Pro Quo (Bonus)

## Definition

Quid pro quo attacks involve offering a service or benefit in exchange for information or access.

### Example

An attacker offers "technical support" and asks the victim to install remote-access software.

## Prevention

* Accept technical support only from authorized personnel.
* Verify support requests through official helpdesk channels.
* Prohibit unauthorized remote-access tools.

---

# Comparison of Social Engineering Attacks

| Attack Type  | Primary Target           | Psychological Lever  | Best Countermeasure           |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | -------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Phishing     | Employees, customers     | Urgency, fear, trust | MFA and phishing awareness    |
| Pretexting   | Employees, support staff | Authority and trust  | Identity verification         |
| Baiting      | Curious users            | Curiosity and reward | Device and download control   |
| Quid Pro Quo | Employees needing help   | Reciprocity          | Authorized support procedures |

---

# Organizational Security Awareness Checklist

Organizations should implement the following measures:

1. Conduct mandatory security awareness training at least annually.
2. Run simulated phishing exercises regularly.
3. Require MFA for critical systems.
4. Establish clear reporting procedures for suspicious contacts.
5. Teach employees to verify identity before sharing information or granting access.

---

# Conclusion

Social engineering attacks remain one of the most successful cyberattack methods because they exploit human behavior rather than technical weaknesses. Phishing, pretexting, baiting, and quid pro quo attacks can lead to credential theft, financial fraud, malware infection, and unauthorized access. The most effective defense is a combination of employee awareness, strong authentication, verification procedures, and a security-focused organizational culture.
